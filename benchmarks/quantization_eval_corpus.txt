The lighthouse stood at the far end of the point, where the gravel road gave way to salt grass and then to bare rock. Every morning before sunrise, the keeper climbed the spiral stair to check the lamp, counting the steps out of habit rather than necessity, since she had climbed them so many times that her feet knew the way better than her eyes did in the dark. The lamp itself was old but reliable, a heavy brass fixture that had been rebuilt twice since the lighthouse was first lit, and she took a quiet pride in keeping it clean, the way some people take pride in a garden or a well-kept kitchen.

Winters here were long, and the wind off the water could knock a grown man flat if he wasn't careful. The keeper had grown used to it, the same way she had grown used to the low groan the tower made when a storm pressed against it from the north. She kept a logbook, as keepers before her had done for more than a century, recording the weather, the ships that passed, and anything unusual that happened on the point. Most entries were plain: fog until noon, wind from the southwest, no vessels sighted. Occasionally something worth remembering appeared in the margins, a pod of whales moving south, a fishing boat that had lost its engine and drifted until a passing trawler took it under tow.

The town below the point was small, little more than a harbor, a general store, and a scattering of houses along the single road that connected them. Most of the people there made their living from the sea in one way or another, and they understood the lighthouse the way they understood the tides, as something that simply had to be tended if the harbor was going to stay safe. Children from the town sometimes walked out to the point on clear afternoons, daring each other to touch the tower's stone base before running back toward home, and the keeper would watch them from the gallery, remembering when she had done the same thing as a girl, long before she imagined she would ever live there herself.

In the evenings, once the lamp was burning steadily and the logbook was up to date, she would sit on the narrow bench outside the keeper's cottage and watch the light sweep across the water. It was strange, she thought, how something so mechanical could feel almost alive, turning without pause through the night, indifferent to whether anyone was watching. She had read once that a lighthouse keeper's job was really just a long exercise in patience, and she supposed that was true, though patience did not feel like quite the right word for it. It was closer to a kind of steady attention, a willingness to notice small changes before they became large ones, whether in the weather, in the machinery, or in the sea itself.

By the time she climbed back up for her final check of the night, the town below had mostly gone dark, save for a few scattered windows and the lanterns on boats still working their way home. She would stand at the top for a moment longer than necessary, watching the horizon for anything out of place, and then she would go back down, satisfied that the light was doing exactly what it was built to do.
